How we calculate the share of non-EU parts

The methodology for the 35 % rule in SAFE, EDIP and related regulations. We work from the wording of the regulations and from the method the European Commission published for EDIP. Where the rules are silent, we say so and describe what we do.

01In brief

The 35 % rule says: parts made outside the EU may make up at most 35 % of the cost of the product's parts. This is how we calculate it:

  1. We take the parts you buy directly for the product, and their prices.
  2. For each part we establish the country of origin and the document that confirms it.
  3. We divide the cost of parts originating outside the EU by the cost of all parts.
  4. We compare the result with the 35 % limit. In the worst case, a part without a document counts as a non-EU part.

“Outside the EU” is a simplification. Each programme has its own list of countries that count as home countries. For SAFE, they include Norway, Iceland, Liechtenstein and Ukraine. See the overview under Rules and home countries.

02Wording of the rule

The cost of components originating outside the Union, EEA EFTA States and Ukraine shall not be higher than 35 % of the estimated cost of the components of the end product.

Council Regulation (EU) 2025/1106 (SAFE), Article 16(10) [1]

SAFE also has a separate condition: no part may originate from a third country that is contrary to the security and defence interests of the EU.

Under SAFE recital 19, the Commission “could establish guidance” on the calculation. As of 18 October 2026 it has not done so for SAFE.

The same rule in other regulations

  • EDIP, Regulation (EU) 2025/2643, Article 10(3) and (4): parts originating “outside the Union and associated countries”, the same 35 % limit. Under recital 27, raw materials are not considered parts. [2]
  • EDIP Ukraine Support Instrument (USI), Article 27: parts originating “outside the Union and Ukraine”. [2]
  • Ukraine loan, Regulation (EU) 2026/467, Article 13(4)(g): the same as SAFE. [3]
  • EU–Canada agreement on participation in SAFE, Article 5(8), applicable from 1 August 2026. Parts from Canada may exceed 35 %. Parts from the EU, the EEA EFTA states (Norway, Iceland, Liechtenstein) and Ukraine must make up at least 20 %. Parts from other third countries at most 35 %, and no more than the EU share. [4]

What is at stake if not met

Under SAFE, suspension of payment; under EDIP, reduction or termination of the grant. False information can lead to exclusion from EU funding.

03How we calculate

The Commission published the detailed method for EDIP in call EDF-EDIP-P-2026-LS-IRA (version 1.2 of 10 June 2026, pages 9–10) and in the training slides “Origin of Components” (5/2026) [5] [6]. We use it for EDIP, and for SAFE until the Commission issues SAFE guidance.

1. What a part is

The regulations speak of components. The Commission means substances, materials, parts, assemblies and software incorporated into the product. Raw materials are not included. On our website we use the simpler word part.

2. Which parts count

Only parts you buy directly, that is from your own suppliers. The Commission calls them tier-1. How important the part is does not matter. Parts your suppliers buy are not counted separately. They are reflected in the origin of the part you buy from them.

3. How origin is determined

  • Imported part: from customs documents or the purchase contract.
  • Part bought in the EU (also through a distributor or within a group): the country where the part underwent its last substantial, economically justified transformation decides. This is the non-preferential origin under Article 60 of the Union Customs Code [7]. The supplier knows it, so the supplier confirms it with a declaration.

4. Which price

The price from invoices, contracts or customs documents, excluding transport, duties and taxes. The cost of an item is the quantity per product × unit price × exchange rate to the project currency.

5. Two calculation methods

Both methods have the same numerator: the cost of purchased non-EU parts. They differ in the denominator.

Method (a) purchased non-EU partsall purchased parts≤ 35 %
Method (b) purchased non-EU partspurchased parts + in-house sub-assemblies≤ 35 %

Method (b) adds to the denominator the total manufacturing cost of the sub-assemblies you make yourself. This covers labour and overheads. Purchased parts that are already on the list are not counted twice, and final assembly is not counted at all. If you make no sub-assemblies in-house, method (b) does not apply.

Meeting one method is enough. But the condition must hold for the whole project: “The condition must be complied with during the entire duration of the action.”

04The Commission's example

The Commission's slides calculate one product with both methods [6]. Purchased non-EU parts cost 92, all purchased parts 214 and in-house sub-assemblies 61. Method (a) gives 92 / 214 = 43 % and fails. Method (b) gives 92 / 275 = 34 % and passes. The slides round; the exact results are 42.99 % and 33.45 %.

06Documents and scenarios

Without supporting records the condition is hard to verify. The European Court of Auditors points this out too [8]. So for each item we distinguish three states:

Documented
The country of origin is filled in and the evidence is a customs document, a supplier declaration or a purchase contract. The declaration has not yet expired.
Stated, not documented
The country of origin is filled in, but the evidence is missing, is only an assumption, or the declaration has expired.
Unknown
The country of origin is missing.

From these states we calculate three scenarios. So you see the result as a range, not as a single number.

Worstaudit
Only documented items with home origin count as home. Everything else counts as non-EU parts. This is how the result looks in a check that accepts documents only.
Expected
The stated origin is used, otherwise the expected origin, otherwise the supplier's country. Items with no information at all count as non-EU parts.
Best
The stated origin is used, and items of unknown origin count as home. This shows the ceiling: with the current parts it cannot turn out better.

We calculate each scenario with both methods. The verdict follows from the results:

  • Compliant the worst scenario passes under at least one method.
  • Depends on evidence the worst scenario fails, the expected or best one passes. The documents decide.
  • Not compliant even the best scenario fails. Documents will not help; some non-EU parts need to be replaced.

Headroom tells you how much more non-EU cost you can add: 0.35 × denominator − numerator. We calculate it for the more favourable method in the worst and the expected scenario. Negative headroom is the amount that is missing.

What “documented” means

A valid document of origin exists for the item. It is not a legal confirmation of origin. The supplier who signed the declaration is responsible for its content. Your company is responsible for the information submitted to the contracting authority or the Commission.

07Minimum evidence set

You do not have to chase every supplier. We look for the smallest number of items whose documentation is enough for even the worst scenario to pass.

  1. We take the undocumented items where we expect home origin. Only for these is it worth chasing a document.
  2. We sort them from the most expensive and add them until the worst scenario passes: numerator − documented cost ≤ 0.35 × denominator. Sorting from the most expensive gives the smallest possible number of items.
  3. We do this for both methods and pick the one with the smaller set.
  4. We group the items by supplier. We contact the suppliers in the set first.

If even all such items are not enough, documents alone will not help. We then calculate a replacement: the smallest number of items of non-EU origin that need to be replaced with parts of home origin for at least the expected scenario to pass. We start with the most expensive.

08Five part categories

The Commission's “Procurement strategy” template, which is part of the EDIP application, asks for the share broken down into five categories. We sort the output the same way. Items without a category are listed separately.

  • Structural and mechanical
  • Power and energy
  • Instrumentation and monitoring
  • Safety and protection
  • Electronics and comms

For each category we give the total cost, the cost of non-EU parts (expected scenario), the undocumented cost, the non-EU share within the category and its contribution to the overall share. Only this summary goes into the application, not the individual items.

The template also asks for a description of the approach. The Commission itself mentions “collection and verification of supplier declarations and customs documents, inclusion of origin clauses in contracts”. That is exactly what we do.

09Open questions

Some things are not expressly covered by the regulations or by the Commission's method. Here is what we do. We record every choice in the description of the approach, so it can be traced.

SAFE has no method of its own yet

We calculate using the EDIP approach, because the wording of the rule is the same. We report the result under both methods. When the Commission issues SAFE guidance, we recalculate the result from the same data.

Transport and duty in in-house production

Purchased parts are counted without transport, duties and taxes. But according to the Commission, the total manufacturing cost of in-house sub-assemblies includes inbound transport and non-recoverable duty. The method does not explain this difference. So we count in-house production as the Commission defines it and show the transport and duty in it separately. If this changed the verdict, we show the result in both variants.

Raw material or material

Raw materials do not count (EDIP recital 27), materials do. Where exactly the line runs, for example for semi-finished products, has to be assessed item by item. We flag and justify every item excluded as a raw material. For borderline items we calculate both variants. Until we have support for the more favourable one, the less favourable one applies.

A contract covering several product variants

The rule speaks of a product. When a contract covers several variants or configurations, we calculate each one separately and point out the one that comes out worst. We do not add up or average variants unless the contracting authority confirms that approach.

Validity for the whole project

Declarations based on our template are valid for at most 24 months, and in them the supplier commits to reporting any change. Expired declarations count as undocumented. We warn you in advance about declarations that expire within 90 days.

Exchange rates

We agree the exchange rates with you in advance and record them in the description of the approach.

10What the output is and is not

  • The output is a calculation based on documents and supplier declarations, together with a description of the approach. We do not give legal opinions.
  • Disputed origin cases are resolved with a customs specialist.
  • A declaration based on our template is a declaration of non-preferential origin. It is not a preferential declaration under Implementing Regulation (EU) 2015/2447.
  • You can repeat the calculation at any time in the calculator. It follows the rules described on this page.
